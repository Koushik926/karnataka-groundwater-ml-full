## Module 1 — prediction

15 features: 4 lagged levels, rolling mean, rolling standard deviation (all per station), month as sine and cosine, latitude, longitude, plus 5 rainfall features (annual total, monsoon total of the last **completed** season, 12-month anomaly, deviation from long-run mean, monsoon share). Missing values imputed with the **training-split** median only.

| Split | Rows | Period |
|---|---|---|
| Train | 79,218 | 1996-05-15 to 2017-05-15 |
| Validation | 16,975 | 2017-05-15 to 2020-11-30 |
| Test (held out) | 16,976 | 2020-11-30 to 2024-08-25 |

Split is **chronological**, not random: the lag features would otherwise put a station's future on both sides of the split and the model would score well by memorising.

| Model | Val RMSE (m) | Val MAE (m) | Val R2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random Forest | 3.9216 | 2.3868 | 0.6941 |
| Xgboost **(selected)** | 3.9131 | 2.3486 | 0.6954 |
| Svr | 5.8963 | 3.6737 | 0.3084 |
| Baseline Last Value | 4.9612 | 3.1169 | 0.5104 |

**Held-out test result: RMSE 3.136 m, MAE 1.986 m, R2 0.632**, against a persistence baseline of RMSE 3.838 m and R2 0.449 — an 18% RMSE reduction over 'next quarter equals this quarter'.

Rainfall A/B test (`experiments/ab_rainfall.py`), identical model and split with and without the five rainfall features: test R2 0.576 to 0.632, RMSE 3.365 m to 3.136 m. Running it exposed a leak — `rain_monsoon` was summing the in-progress monsoon, so an August observation carried September's rain.

R2 ~0.63 is consistent with Alkon et al. (2024), who report 0.4–0.6 on the same CGWB data. Higher published figures (0.85–0.90) use monthly or daily data at known stations over short horizons, not a future-period holdout.

Trends: Mann-Kendall **with tie correction** plus Sen's slope, both implemented from first principles. Of 2,042 stations with sufficient record: **312 declining, 460 recovering, 1,270 no significant trend** at p < 0.05. Exhaustive, sums to 2,042.

## Module 2 — risk mapping

Risk assessed for **177 taluks**, 176 matched to a CGWB category. Taluk, not district, because CGWB assesses taluks — validation is one-to-one and resolution is about 6x better.

Five measured indicators weighted by AHP on Saaty's 1–9 scale; weights are the principal eigenvector; **consistency ratio 0.0542**, below the 0.10 the method requires. Classified with Jenks natural breaks (minimises within-class variance, so boundaries fall at real gaps rather than round numbers).

Normalisation was chosen by measurement, not convention (`experiments/normalisation_choice.py`): binary agreement 77.3% for min-max against 67.6% for percentile-clipping and 63.6% for rank.

The decline-rate indicator comes from the **measured** Sen's slope, not from the model's own forecast. Recursive forecasts drift to the training mean — 76% did, correlation with last observed depth -0.665 — which inverted the ranking and put over-exploited Chitradurga at lowest risk.

## Validation against CGWB 2020

| Measure | Value | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| Four-class agreement | 23.9% (kappa 0.001) | Relative Jenks classes vs absolute CGWB thresholds — cannot align by construction |
| Binary agreement | 77.3% | Our High/Very High vs CGWB critical/over-exploited |
| Precision | 0.66 | Of the taluks we flag, how many CGWB also flags |
| Recall | 0.45 | Of the taluks CGWB flags, how many we catch |
| Spearman rank | 0.276 | Whether the ranking order agrees |
| Confusion matrix | TP 23, FP 12, FN 28, TN 113 | The counts behind the above |

**The two weak numbers, and the honest answers.**

1. *Four-class agreement 23.9%, kappa 0.001.* Jenks classes are relative to Karnataka's own distribution; CGWB's categories are absolute national thresholds on stage of extraction. A relative quartile cannot match an absolute cut-off. The fair comparison is binary, 77.3%. Both are reported, never only the flattering one.

2. *Recall 0.45.* The 28 stressed taluks we miss are shallow (5.25 m mean) and flat despite 122% mean extraction; the 23 we catch are deep (11.69 m) and declining. CGWB's monitored shallow wells refill each monsoon while the borewells causing the stress draw from a deeper aquifer. It is a limit of the monitoring network, not a tuning problem. Closing it needs piezometer data, which is the first Phase-2 item.

## How the two modules are coupled

Be precise about this - an examiner may ask to see the line. Two couplings:

1. **By order.** `src/main.py` runs Module 1, then checks it produced trends and stops with an error if not, so Module 2 never scores data Module 1 failed to produce.
2. **By a shared function.** `station_trends()` in `src/data_processor.py` - Mann-Kendall with tie correction plus Sen's slope - is what both modules rely on.

The paths differ: `create_district_indicators()` takes Module 1's forecast trends directly as an argument, while the taluk path - the one producing the delivered risk map - calls `station_trends()` on the same cleaned observations rather than reading `outputs/module1_station_forecasts.csv`. The values are identical (same function, same data). Making the taluk path consume the file too is a Phase-2 tidy-up. **Do not claim the taluk risk map reads Module 1's output file** - it recomputes.

### Research gaps

**Gap 1: Many studies use older observation periods (addressed in Module 1)**

- Evidence: Several key studies stop at 2013–2015 [3], [12], [19], [20], and national forecasts end with data up to 2018 [13]. Recent drought and recovery years in Karnataka are therefore not reflected in current models.
- How we address it: Module 1 builds an up-to-date dataset (latest available CGWB well levels, IMD rainfall and temperature, recent LULC) for Karnataka, studies the trends, and trains and compares ML/DL models to predict future groundwater levels, keeping the most recent seasons for testing.

**Gap 2: Prediction and risk mapping are usually treated separately (addressed in Module 2)**

- Evidence: Risk and vulnerability maps are static and do not use predicted trends [1], [5], [14]. Forecasting studies stop at well-level predictions and do not produce risk zones [13], [15], [17], [18].
- How we address it: Module 2 uses the predicted groundwater trends from Module 1, combines them with environmental factors, and classifies taluks/districts into Low, Moderate, High and Very High depletion-risk zones, validated against CGWB assessment categories and shown on an interactive map.

**Gap 3: Spatial relationships are mostly ignored in Indian studies**

- Evidence: Indian ML studies predict each well independently [13], [17], [18]. Spatio-temporal models such as GCN-LSTM and ConvLSTM have been tested only on small well networks outside India [7], [8].
- How we address it: Not a separate module. If time permits, a spatio-temporal model (ConvLSTM or GCN-LSTM) can be tried in Module 1 as an extension; otherwise it is kept as future scope.

The three papers that matter most in a viva: **Alkon et al. 2024** (same CGWB data, Random Forest + SHAP, R2 0.4–0.6 — sets the realistic bar), **Supreetha et al. 2020** (LSTM on Udupi, Karnataka — deep learning works here but one district, prediction only), **Tao et al. 2022** (review: tree ensembles competitive on tabular well data, and most studies never test against a naive baseline).

## Design decisions, with the alternative rejected

| Decision | Chosen | Rejected, and why |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of risk | Taluk (177) | District — CGWB assesses taluks, so validation would not be one-to-one, and resolution ~6x worse |
| Trend statistic | Mann-Kendall + Sen's slope | The model's own recursive forecast — mean-reverts, inverted the ranking |
| Learning algorithm | XGBoost on validation RMSE | LSTM — tabular, quarterly, 15 features; SVR scored worst (val R2 0.308) |
| Normalisation | Min-max | Percentile-clip (67.6%) and rank (63.6%) vs min-max 77.3% |
| Data split | Chronological | Random k-fold — leaks the future through lag features |
| Storage | CSV/JSON in `outputs/`, `models/` | A database — batch job, single writer, every stage stays inspectable |
